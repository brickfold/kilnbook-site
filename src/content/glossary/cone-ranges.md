[Cone numbers](/glossary/cone-numbers) are often grouped into three ranges. Every clay body is made to mature in one of them, and glazes are made to melt in one of them, so knowing a piece's range tells you which kiln load it belongs in.

| Range | Typical glaze cones | Approximate temperature | Common clay |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Low fire | Cone 06 to 04 | 1,830 to 1,940°F | Earthenware, terracotta |
| Mid-range | Cone 5 to 6 | 2,170 to 2,230°F | Mid-fire stoneware and porcelain |
| High fire | Cone 9 to 10 | 2,300 to 2,350°F | High-fire stoneware and porcelain |

Temperatures vary with how fast the kiln heats, so treat them as a guide.

## Why the range matters

Clay and glaze have to match. A low-fire clay taken to cone 10 can slump, bloat or melt onto the shelf. A cone 10 glaze fired at cone 6 will come out dry and underfired. Most studio mishaps of this kind come from a piece going into the wrong load, not from a bad firing.

## Running more than one range

Many studios offer two ranges, most often cone 6 and cone 10, and that means two glaze queues competing for kiln time. The usual fixes are labelling every piece with its clay body or range when it enters the queue, and agreeing how each range gets scheduled: fixed firing days, firing once a load is full, or a mix of both. Our journal post on [sharing one glaze kiln between cone 6 and cone 10 potters](/blog/glaze-firing-schedules) looks at how three studios do it.
