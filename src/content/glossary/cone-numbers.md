Potters describe firings in cones rather than degrees. A pyrometric cone is a small, slender pyramid made of ceramic materials, designed to soften and bend once it has absorbed a set amount of heat. Each cone number bends at a different point, so "fire to cone 6" means "fire until a cone 6 would bend".

## Heatwork, not just temperature

Clay and glaze respond to both how hot the kiln gets and how long it stays there. A slow firing reaches the same result at a slightly lower peak temperature than a fast one. Cones capture both, which is why they are more useful than a temperature reading alone. Any temperature you see next to a cone number is an approximation for a particular heating rate.

## Reading the numbers

The scale runs from cone 022, the coolest, up through cone 01, then from cone 1 to cone 14.

- **A leading zero means cooler.** Cone 06 and cone 6 are very different firings. Cone 06 is about 1,830°F, a typical bisque. Cone 6 is about 2,230°F, a typical mid-range glaze firing.
- **With a zero, higher numbers are cooler.** Cone 04 is hotter than cone 06.
- **Without a zero, higher numbers are hotter.** Cone 10, at about 2,350°F, is hotter than cone 6.

Mixing up 06 and 6 on a label or a load sheet is an easy mistake and an expensive one, so it is worth saying the "oh" out loud: "cone oh-six".

## Where cone numbers show up

Clay bodies and glazes are sold with the cone they are meant to be fired to, kiln controllers are programmed by cone, and every firing log should record the cone each load went to. Kilnbook's firing log records the date, cone, schedule and who loaded the kiln for every firing. [How firing queues work](/features/firings).
