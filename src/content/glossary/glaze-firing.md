A glaze firing (or glost firing) is the firing that finishes a piece. Glaze applied to bisqueware melts into glass, and the clay body underneath is taken to the temperature it was designed for, where it becomes dense and, for most stoneware and porcelain, close to watertight.

Glaze firings go hotter than [bisque firings](/glossary/bisque-firing). How hot depends on the clay and glazes your studio uses: most community studios glaze fire at mid-range (cone 5 or 6), high fire (cone 10) or both. See [cone ranges](/glossary/cone-ranges).

## Loading a glaze kiln

Glaze is liquid glass at the top of a firing, and anything it touches gets stuck to it. So a glaze load follows stricter rules than a bisque load:

- Pieces must not touch each other.
- The foot of every piece, and the bottom few millimetres of the wall, should be wiped clean of glaze.
- Shelves are coated with [kiln wash](/glossary/kiln-wash) so a drip can be chipped off instead of ruining the shelf.

That spacing means a glaze load holds fewer pieces than a bisque load of the same kiln, which is why glaze queues tend to back up first.

## Glaze firing in a shared studio

A glaze firing is the last thing standing between a member and their finished piece, so it is where waiting feels longest. If your studio fires more than one cone, each one needs its own queue, and members need to know which queue their piece is in.

Kilnbook gives each kiln its own queue for bisque, cone 6 and cone 10 glaze firings, shows how full each load is, and texts members when a load is unloaded. [How firing queues work](/features/firings).
