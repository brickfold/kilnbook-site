A glaze firing (also called a glost firing) takes glazed [bisqueware](/glossary/bisqueware) to the temperature where the glaze melts into glass and the clay body reaches its final strength. For most studio work that means a mid-range firing at [cone 6](/glossary/cone-6) or a high-fire firing at [cone 10](/glossary/cone-10).

## How a glaze load is packed

Glaze melts, so a glaze load is packed very differently from a bisque load:

- Pieces cannot touch each other, or they fuse together.
- The foot of each piece has to be clean of glaze, or it fuses to the kiln shelf.
- Shelves are usually coated with kiln wash to protect them from drips.

That means a glaze load holds much less work than a bisque load, and one badly waxed foot can cost a shelf.

## What it means for the studio

Studios that work at more than one cone have to decide when each glaze load fires: on fixed days, once it is full enough, or a mix of both. Mixing clay bodies is the most common cause of a ruined load, so it pays to keep each cone in its own queue.

In Kilnbook, each kiln has separate queues for bisque, cone 6 and cone 10 glaze firings, and a firing log that records the date, cone, schedule and who loaded it.
