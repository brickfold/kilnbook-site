Ask ten studio owners which electric kiln is best and you will get ten brand names. Ask them what they wish they had checked before buying, and the answers line up much more closely. The right kiln for a home potter is often the wrong one for a studio with members, classes and a queue of greenware on the shelf.

Here is what to work through before you compare models.

## Start with your firing volume, not the kiln

Before you look at a single spec sheet, count what you actually fire. Look at a normal month:

- How many bisque loads and how many glaze loads?
- How full is each load when it goes in?
- How long does work wait on the shelf before it fires?
- Which weeks back up: the end of a class session, the run-up to a holiday sale?

If pieces regularly wait more than a week, you probably need more kiln capacity. If loads routinely go in half full, a bigger kiln will make the wait worse, not better. A second, smaller kiln is often the better buy than one large one.

## Size: bigger is not always better

A large kiln holds more per firing, but it takes longer to fill. In a studio, that is the number that matters to members: the time between setting a bowl on the shelf and picking it up.

Things to weigh:

- **Fill time.** Estimate how many days of member work it takes to fill each size you are considering.
- **The work your members make.** Tall vases and large platters need interior height and shelf width. Mugs and bowls pack well in almost anything.
- **Loading.** Deep top-loaders are hard on backs. If staff or members load the kiln, have them try reaching the bottom shelf before you commit.

## Cone rating: match your clay bodies

Most community studios fire to cone 6 for glaze, and plenty of electric kilns are built around that range. If some members work in higher-fire clay bodies, check the kiln's maximum rating and how hard it will be working to reach it. A kiln that fires near its limit every load will wear its elements faster.

If you run both mid-range and high-fire work, decide whether one kiln will carry both or whether each gets its own. We wrote about [sharing one kiln between two cones](/blog/glaze-firing-schedules) if you are weighing that up.

## Power and the room it goes in

This is where studio purchases most often stall. Before you order:

- Confirm the electrical supply in the building with a licensed electrician: voltage, phase and the circuit the kiln needs.
- Plan venting. A kiln room without proper ventilation is a problem for members, staff and your landlord.
- Check clearances around the kiln and what is on the other side of the wall.
- Ask your insurer whether a new kiln changes your policy.

Getting this wrong can cost more than the kiln itself, so do it first.

## Controllers and firing records

A digital controller with saved programs lets more than one person fire the kiln without guesswork. For a shared studio, look for a controller that is easy to read, can store your regular bisque and glaze schedules, and makes it obvious when a firing has finished or stopped early.

Whatever you buy, keep a firing log: the date, cone, schedule and who loaded it. When a glaze comes out wrong or an element starts to slow down, that log is the first thing you will want.

## Running costs to budget for

The purchase price is only the start. Budget for:

- Replacement elements, relays and thermocouples
- Kiln shelves and posts (and the occasional shelf lost to a glaze run)
- Kiln wash
- Electricity for each firing

Those costs are part of what your firing fees should cover. Charging by cubic inch ties the fee to how much kiln space each piece takes up, which is fairer to members than a flat price per pot.
